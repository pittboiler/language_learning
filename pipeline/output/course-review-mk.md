### Am / is / are: сум

**Agenda:** Say am, is, are: сум, си, е and friends

**Rule card:** сум means “am”, and it changes with the person: јас сум, ти си, тој/таа е, ние сме, вие сте, тие се. It never starts a sentence; something always comes first: Добро сум (“I'm well”), Јас сум Ана.

**Recap:** The verb for am/is/are changes with who you mean: сум (I), си (you), е (he/she/it), сме (we), сте (you all, or polite you), се (they). It's a light word that leans on the word before it, so it never opens a sentence: Добро сум, фала. Because the form already says who, you can usually drop јас: Добро сум is a complete “I'm well”. You'll hear it all through this chapter: Ана е во Скопје (“Ana is in Skopje”), Од Германија сум (“I'm from Germany”).

**Examples:**
- Јас сум Ана — *I am Ana* `grammar:to-be#0`
- „Добро сум.“ — *"I'm well."* `story:gen-s0-greet-story#3`
- Марко е доктор. — *Marko is a doctor.* `qa:gen-s1-greet-intro-story#q2:a`
- Ние сме тука — *We are here* `grammar:to-be#2`

<details><summary>Library page</summary>

The present tense of “to be” has six forms: сум (I am), си (you are), е (he/she/it is), сме (we are), сте (you all are, or polite you are), се (they are). These forms are unstressed: they lean on the word before them, so they can't start a sentence. Put a subject or another word first: Јас сум Ана, Тоа е кафе, Добро сум. Since the form already shows the person, the pronoun is usually left out unless you want to stress it. To make it negative, put не in front: не е, не сум.

**Why it's like this**
- Why can't сум start a sentence? It's unstressed, so it needs a word in front to lean on. That's why it's Добро сум (“I'm well”), with сум second.
- Why is јас often missing? сум can only mean “I am”, so јас is added only for emphasis or contrast: И јас сум добро (“I'm fine too”).
- Watch the look-alike: се means “they are” here, but the same little word also appears in Се гледаме (“See you”). Chapter 5 explains that second job.
- сте is both “you all are” and the polite “you are” to one person, so it pairs with вие either way (chapter 2 covers ти and вие).

**Common mistakes**
- Not opening with сум, but leaning it on a word first: Добро сум, Јас сум Ана.
- Not using сум for everyone: “you are” is си (or сте when polite), “she is” is е.
- Not adding a pronoun every time: Добро сум already means “I'm well”.

</details>

### Saying “not”: не

**Agenda:** Say “not”: put не right before the verb

**Rule card:** To make a sentence negative, put не directly in front of the verb: Не разбирам (“I don't understand”), Не знам (“I don't know”). Nothing goes between не and the verb.

**Recap:** Today's rule is one small word: не, right before the verb. You used it in the two phrases a beginner needs most, Не разбирам and Не знам. The verb stays exactly as it was; не just sits in front of it. It works the same way with “is”: Тоа не е точно means “That isn't right”. There's no extra word like English “do”: “I don't know” is simply Не знам.

**Examples:**
- „Извинете, не разбирам.“ — *"Excuse me, I don't understand."* `story:gen-s0-repair-story#2`
- Не разбирам. — *I don't understand.* `qa:gen-s0-repair-story#q1:a`
- Тоа не е точно — *That isn't right* `grammar:negation#1`
- Не, Марко не е тука сега. — *No, Marko is not here now.* `scenario:gen-phone#2`

<details><summary>Library page</summary>

Macedonian makes almost any sentence negative with one small word, не, placed immediately before the verb: разбирам → не разбирам, знам → не знам, сакам → не сакам. With am/is/are it goes in front of that word too: Тоа не е точно (“That isn't right”). не and its verb are said together, in one breath, with no pause between them. On its own at the start of a reply, Не simply means “No”: Не, фала (“No, thanks”).

**Why it's like this**
- Why right before the verb? Macedonian treats не and the verb as a pair said in one breath, so не always sits directly in front of it: Не разбирам.
- Two jobs for one word: on its own at the start of a reply, Не means “No”; in front of a verb it means “not”. You can hear both in Не, сѐ уште учи (“No, she's still learning”).
- There's no helper word like English “do”: “I don't know” is just Не знам, literally “not I-know”.
- For “there isn't” or “don't have”, Macedonian uses a different word, нема (Нема проблем, “No problem”). That one comes in chapter 12; for now, treat Нема проблем as a set phrase.

**Common mistakes**
- Not putting не after the verb, but before it: Не разбирам.
- Not adding an extra word for “do”: “I don't understand” is just Не разбирам.
- Not reading every Не as “not”: at the start of an answer, Не, фала means “No, thanks”.

</details>

**Grammar cards**

### Question words

**Agenda:** Ask what, where, how, how much: question words go first

**Rule card:** Start the question with the question word: Што значи …? (“What does … mean?”), Како се вели …? (“How do you say …?”), Каде е Марко? (“Where is Marko?”). The question word up front is all it takes.

**Recap:** You've been asking Што значи …? and Како се вели …? since chapter 1; now here's how they work. Questions that ask for information rather than yes or no begin with a question word: што (what), кој (who), каде (where), кога (when), зошто (why), како (how), колку (how much / how many). - Not leaving the question word at the end, as in English “You're from where?”: it goes first, Од каде сте?

### Casual ти or polite вие

**Agenda:** Choose ти for friends, вие for strangers and elders

**Rule card:** Macedonian has two words for “you”. Use ти with friends and family (Како си?) and вие with strangers, elders and groups (Како сте?). The verb matches: polite forms end in -те.

**Recap:** Today you chose between two “you”s. ти is the casual one: Здраво, Марко! Како си? вие is polite (and also “you all”): Добар ден! Како сте? The verb follows your choice, so polite forms end in -те: сте, Сакате ли кафе?, and the Можете ли да повторите? you've used since chapter 1. Notice the pairing in the greetings too: a casual Здраво goes with ти, a polite Добар ден with вие. With a stranger, start with вие.

### Want to, can: да + verb

**Agenda:** Say want to / can / must: stack да + verb (Сакам да платам).

**Rule card:** Macedonian has no "to eat" word. Instead you say да plus a normal verb, after words like сакам (want), можам (can), морам (must), треба (need): „Сакам да учам македонски“ — I want to learn Macedonian.

Only треба stays frozen (треба да…). This little да pattern runs through almost every real sentence you'll say in a café.

**Examples:**

<details><summary>Library page</summary>

English uses an "infinitive" (to pay, to go) after want/can/must. Macedonian has none. Instead you take a helper word — сакам (want), можам/може (can), морам (must), треба (need) — add да, then a fully conjugated verb: Сакам да платам = I want (I) to pay. The helper and the second verb both match the same person: сакам да јадам (I…I), сакаш да јадеш (you…you). **Why it's like this**
- There's no infinitive at all, so you can't say "to pay" as one word — да + a conjugated verb does that whole job.
- Both verbs carry a person ending, and they must agree: Сакам да платам, never a mismatched pair. The да just links them.
- треба is impersonal — it doesn't change for who: „Треба да јадам/учам." Compare сакам/морам, which do change.
- Questions can drop ли in relaxed speech: Можам да платам? said with a rising voice means “Can I pay?”.

**Common mistakes**
- Not leaving out да: two verbs always need да between them, as in Сакам да платам.
- Not freezing the second verb: it takes the same person as the first, so сакаш goes with да јадеш, сакам with да јадам.
- Not dropping да in a question: it stays right before the action verb, as in Можете ли да повторите?

</details>

Macedonian has two simple past tenses, and at this stage you can treat them as one family. **Why it's like this**
- Why do беше and отиде end differently if both mean “he/she …”? They're the two kinds of past: -ше forms describe states and ongoing actions (беше, имаше, јадеше), the short stem a single finished event (отиде). Both are shared by “you” and “he/she”.
- English splits "I was doing" from "I did", but here one past form often covers both: "Што правеше?" can be "What were you doing?" or "What did you do?" depending on the moment.
- The -в of "I" and the -вме of "we" are easy to hear — they both have that в sound. The "they" form instead doubles the vowel into -а, as in "тие гледаа телевизија".

**Common mistakes**
- Not using the present ending when the action is finished, but the past ending — "гледав" for a film you already watched, not the "I watch" form, because the event is over.
- Not adding anything to tell “you” from “he/she”: one form serves both (беше, отиде), and the rest of the sentence makes it clear.
- Not mixing up who did it: with a name in the sentence like "Марко беше дома", the bare form is "he", so read the whole sentence before deciding.

</details>

**Grammar cards**
- 🃏 *How do you make the past "I" form?* → Add -в to the verb stem. (e.g. гледав филм)
- 🃏 *What's different about the past "you" and "he/she" forms?* → They look exactly the same — context tells you who. (e.g. Каде отиде Марко денес?)

### Two versions of a verb *(recognize only)*

**Agenda:** Spot two forms of each verb: one ongoing, one one-time done.

**Rule card:** Most Macedonian verbs travel in pairs: one form for an action that's ongoing or repeated, another for a single, finished action. Compare "пие кафе секое утро" (every morning) with "го испи кафето" (drank it all up). For now, just notice the difference — you'll learn both halves as you go.

**Recap:** Every verb you meet really comes as a pair. One member is for habits and things in progress — "пие кафе секое утро", drinks coffee every morning. - Отидов во кафуле и јадев сендвич. — *I went to a café and ate a sandwich.* `scenario:gen-s2-pasttime#3`

<details><summary>Library page</summary>

Macedonian verbs usually come in two-member pairs (verb aspect: imperfective / perfective). English leans on extra words and tense to show this ("was reading" vs "read it all"), but Macedonian often has two related verb forms instead, such as пие / испие for "drink". **Why it's like this**
- English speakers expect one verb plus helper words; here the choice is baked into which member of the pair you use, so the same idea has two base verbs.
- The completed form is natural for a single finished event ("го испи кафето"), while the ongoing form fits a repeated routine ("пие кафе секое утро") — the same scene, told two ways.
- In the past tense this shows up clearly: a background, in-progress action like "јадев" versus a single completed move like "отидов".

**Common mistakes**
- Not treating the two forms as unrelated words, but seeing them as two halves of one pair that share a meaning.
- Not using the completed form for a habit like every-morning coffee, but the ongoing form, because the action repeats.

</details>

### There is, there isn't: има and нема

**Agenda:** Say there is / there isn't with има and нема (and double negatives).

**Rule card:** Use има for "there is / there's a…" and нема for "there isn't / there's no…": „Има проблем“ vs „Нема проблем“. With нема-type words, Macedonian doubles the negative: „Немам ништо“ = I have nothing.

**Recap:** има means "there is" and нема means "there isn't" — two little words that cover a lot. You've been saying „Нема проблем“ since the very first chapters without thinking about it, and „Што има?“ for "what's up?" — now you can build your own: „Има проблем“, „нема вода“. Remember that Macedonian wants a double negative where English uses just one: „Немам ништо“ is "I have nothing", not "I don't have nothing". нема also doubles as "doesn't have".

**Examples:**
- Извинете, ама има проблем. — *Sorry, but there's a problem.* `story:gen-s2-problems-story#1`
- Добар ден! Има проблем. Кафето не е топло. — *Good day! There is a problem. The coffee is not hot.* `scenario:gen-s2-problems#1`
- Немам ништо — *I have nothing* `grammar:negation#3`
- „Денес нема дожд. Сакам да одам надвор!“ вели Марко. — *"Today there's no rain. I want to go outside!" says Marko.* `story:gen-s2-smalltalk-story#7`

- Таму има автобус за центарот. — *There is a bus to the centre there.* `story:gen-s1-directions-story#5`
- Да, јас сум. Што има? — *Yes, it's me. What's up?* `scenario:gen-s2-arrange#2`

има and нема are the "there is / there isn't" pair, and they also mean "has / doesn't have". For a problem that exists, say „Има проблем“; to say none exists, „Нема проблем“ or „нема вода“. нема is simply the negative partner of има, so you never put не in front of it — нема already carries the "no". A key Macedonian habit: when the sentence is negative, every matching word goes negative too (this is the required "double negative"). So "I have nothing" is „Немам ништо“ — both the verb and "nothing" stay negative together, which to an English ear sounds like "I don't have nothing".

**Why it's like this**
- нема is already не + има squashed together, so you don't add another не — it does the negating by itself.
- English allows only one negative per sentence; Macedonian insists on matching them, so a negative verb takes a negative word alongside it, as in „Немам ништо“.
- „Што има?“ literally uses this same има but lands as the friendly "what's up?" — the same word stretches from "there is" to "what's going on".

**Common mistakes**
- Not putting не before нема, but letting нема stand alone — нема already means "there isn't".
- Not using a single negative like English, but doubling it: „Немам ништо“, because Macedonian requires the negatives to agree.
- Not translating “no problem” word by word: it's simply Нема проблем, literally “there's no problem”.

### The “have done” past *(recognize only)*

**Agenda:** Recognize the "have done" past: сум plus an -л word for experiences.

**Rule card:** For experiences and things you didn't see yourself, Macedonian uses сум plus a word ending in -л: "Сум бил во Скопје" — I've been to Skopje. In he/she/they, the сум drops away and just the -л word remains: "Тој видел сѐ."

**Recap:** You now recognize a second kind of past. The plain past (like "бев", I was) is for things you witnessed; this new one, сум plus an -л word, is for experiences and things you heard about but didn't see. "Дали си јадел баклава?" asks "Have you eaten baklava?" — note the си before the -л word. The -л word shifts for man or woman: "Сум бил" for a man, "Сум била" for a woman. For now just spot it in the wild; you'll build it yourself later.

**Examples:**
- Сум бил во Скопје — *I've been to Skopje (m)* `grammar:perfect-tense#0`
- Сум била во Скопје — *I've been to Skopje (f)* `grammar:perfect-tense#1`
- Дали си јадел баклава? — *Have you eaten baklava?* `grammar:perfect-tense#2`
- Тој видел сѐ — *He has seen everything* `grammar:perfect-tense#3`

<details><summary>Library page</summary>

This is a second past tense (the сум-perfect, built on an l-form). You take the present of сум and add a verb word ending in -л / -ла / -ло / -ле, and that ending agrees with the person speaking: "Сум бил во Скопје" (a man), "Сум била во Скопје" (a woman). With ти it's си: "Дали си јадел баклава?" With ние it's сме биле. The one twist: in he/she/they there is no сум at all — you just use the -л word on its own, as in "Тој видел сѐ" (he has seen everything). Use it for life experiences and for things you didn't witness directly, as opposed to the plain past which reports something you saw happen.

**Why it's like this**
- English uses one word, "have", for everyone; Macedonian changes the helper by person (сум, си, сме) AND changes the -л word for man or woman — two things moving at once.
- The missing helper in he/she/they trips people up: "Тој видел сѐ" has no сум, while "Сум бил" does. The third person stands alone.
- Why two pasts at all? The plain past is for what you saw; this сум-plus-л past leans toward experiences and second-hand news. Both are real pasts, just different flavours.
- Didn't chapter 2 say сум never starts a sentence? That rule is for сум meaning “am” (Добро сум). As the helper in this past, сум can come first: Сум бил во Скопје.

**Common mistakes**
- Not keeping сум in the he/she form, but dropping it — he/she/they use the bare -л word, as in "Тој видел сѐ".
- Not using a man's -л word for a woman, but matching it: "Сум бил" for a man, "Сум била" for a woman.
- Not reaching for this past to report something you actually witnessed — for what you saw happen, the plain past fits better.

</details>

**Grammar cards**
- 🃏 *What two pieces build the "have done" past?* → сум (or си/сме…) plus a word ending in -л/-ла/-ло/-ле. (e.g. Сум бил во Скопје)
- 🃏 *What happens to сум in he/she/they?* → It disappears — just the bare -л word is left. (e.g. Тој видел сѐ)
- ▢ Дали си ___ баклава? → **јадел** of [јадел / бил / видел]: After си, the -л word carries the meaning — here "eaten".
